Lesson 09.1 established the cliff. This one is about the shape you build on the far side of it — and the two facts that decide the bill: switch radix sets your tier count, and LLM traffic breaks the assumption ECMP was designed around.

Part oneradix

Why the tier count is a design variable

A switch has a fixed port count at a given speed — its radix — bounded by the ASIC's total switching capacity divided by the per-port rate. 51.2 Tb/s buys 64 ports at 800G or 128 at 400G. That is the trade, not a choice you escape by buying a bigger box.

The structural version of “Ethernet is cheaper”

Doubling radix from 64 to 128 quadruples what a 2-tier design holds — which is exactly why a higher-radix Ethernet switch can sometimes let you delete a tier. Deleting a tier deletes an entire population of optical transceivers, not just some switches.

That argument is far more durable than any price quote.

Part twooversubscription

The ratio that sets the bill

   ┌────────────────────┐           ┌────────────────────┐
   │  32 up  (12.8 Tb/s)│           │   8 up   (3.2 Tb/s)│
   │  32 down(12.8 Tb/s)│           │  32 down(12.8 Tb/s)│

        ratio 1:1                        ratio 4:1

   Consequences of the 4:1 leaf:
     · a flow between two GPUs on the SAME leaf     → 400 Gb/s
       it never touches an uplink; the ratio is irrelevant
     · all 32 GPUs sending simultaneously OFF-leaf  → 3.2 Tb/s
       shared 32 ways = 100 Gb/s each = line rate / 4
     · cost saved: 24 fewer uplinks per leaf, 48 fewer
       transceivers, a proportionally smaller spine tier
  1. Oversubscription only taxes traffic that crosses the oversubscribed tier

    Intra-leaf traffic is unaffected. This is the entire foundation of the rail-optimised argument.

  2. Worst case per endpoint through an X:1 tier is line rate ÷ X

    And only when every endpoint under the tier is active across it. If a fraction cross, survivors get proportionally more — the ratio is a worst case, not a constant tax.

So the design question is never “full bisection or not”

It is “at which tier can this traffic pattern tolerate oversubscription, and by how much” — and answering it requires knowing the traffic pattern, which for LLM training we do.

A general-purpose cloud cannot oversubscribe aggressively: any VM may talk to any VM, so the traffic matrix is dense and unpredictable. LLM training's is neither.

Heavy collectives are rail-local
  • all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter
  • run GPU-N ↔ GPU-N
  • every participant on the same rail
  • bytes ride one leaf and terminate
NVLink absorbs the residue
  • GPU3 wanting GPU5 crosses rails
  • shuffled sideways inside the node first
  • 450 GB/s against the NIC's 50
  • NCCL implements this as PXN
Which is why the spine can be oversubscribed almost for free

Put those two together and the aggregation tier carries only cross-pod jobs, checkpoints, storage and control traffic — so a ratio like 1:7 there costs the training collectives essentially nothing while deleting a large fraction of the optics bill.

Part threepath selection

ECMP assumes many flows. You have four.

A fat tree gives many equal-cost paths; ECMP hashes packet header fields to choose among them. Hashing rather than round-robin is deliberate — every packet of a flow hashes identically, so packets keep their order, which matters enormously to a transport that recovers from reordering badly.

The mechanism only works if there are many more flows than paths. Here is exactly where that breaks:

   WHAT ECMP WAS DESIGNED FOR          WHAT LLM TRAINING SENDS
   thousands of small, independent,     a handful of huge, synchronised,
   short-lived flows                    long-lived flows

   uplink 0  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  ~equal         uplink 0  ████████████████ 400G
   uplink 1  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  ~equal         uplink 1  ████████████████ 400G
   uplink 2  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  ~equal         uplink 2  (idle)
   uplink 3  ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓  ~equal         uplink 3  (idle)

   law of large numbers                 two hashes collided; half the
   smooths the imbalance                tier is saturated, half idle
The consequence is a silent 2× on a healthy-looking fabric

Every switch counter is green, no link is down, no ratio is violated — and half your uplinks carry nothing while the other half queue. The fix is raising entropy (more QPs per connection) or replacing hashing with adaptive routing, not adding bandwidth.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

How many endpoints fit in a 2-tier fat tree of 64-port switches, and why does it matter?

k²/2 = 2,048. A 24,576-GPU cluster therefore needs three tiers on that switch.

It matters because the third tier is the long-reach one — row to row, hall to hall — and that is where optical transceiver cost concentrates. Higher radix can delete a tier, and deleting a tier deletes a whole population of optics.

What does a 4:1 leaf actually cost a flow?

Nothing at all if both endpoints are on that leaf — the flow never touches an uplink. If all 32 endpoints send off-leaf simultaneously, each gets 3.2 Tb/s ÷ 32 = 100 Gb/s, a quarter of line rate.

The ratio is a worst case, not a constant tax.

Why can an LLM cluster oversubscribe its spine when a general-purpose cloud cannot?

Because the traffic matrix is known and sparse. The bandwidth-dominant collectives run GPU-N ↔ GPU-N, so they are rail-local and terminate at the leaf. Cross-rail residue is shuffled inside the node over NVLink first (NCCL's PXN), so it also leaves on its own rail.

The aggregation tier is then left carrying only cross-pod jobs, checkpoints and storage — which tolerate 1:7 easily.

Where does flow entropy come from on a RoCEv2 fabric?

The source UDP port, which the NIC randomises per queue pair. The destination port is always 4791 — it identifies RoCE, not the flow — so it contributes nothing.

So entropy equals the number of QPs, and NCCL defaults to one QP per connection. One path per peer.

Half your uplinks are saturated and half are idle. Every counter is green. What happened?

ECMP hash collision. With a handful of huge synchronised flows and entropy equal to the QP count, two flows hashed to the same uplink while others got none. The law of large numbers that normally smooths ECMP does not apply at four flows.

The fix is more entropy (QPs per connection) or adaptive routing — not more bandwidth.
