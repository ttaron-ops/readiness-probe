Time-slicing is the easiest sharing mode to turn on and the one whose cost report will quietly lie to you by exactly the sharing factor you chose to save money. The mechanism is simple; the attribution consequence is the lesson.

Part onemechanism

Packing time, and nothing else

The device plugin advertises one physical GPU as N schedulable replicas. Pods are handed annotated IDs of the form GPU-<uuid>::0, ::1, … and the GPU context-switches between them. Three properties follow, and all three are absences.

  1. No memory isolation

    Nothing caps a client's allocation. One pod can consume the entire framebuffer, and its neighbours OOM — but the OOM surfaces in the victim's logs, not the culprit's, which is why the symptom is so confusing.

  2. No fault isolation

    The fault domain is the whole device. An Xid that takes the GPU down takes every sharing pod with it.

  3. No concurrency gain

    Turns are serialised. Each turn still wastes whatever fraction of the SM array the resident job does not need — a 15 GB model on an 80 GB card typically lights a small fraction of the SMs, and under time-slicing that fraction is all the GPU ever does at any instant. Packing time does not pack occupancy. That is MPS's job.

Part twothe attribution hole

The query everyone writes on day one

The sentence to remember

The more aggressively you share to save money, the more your cost report over-states what you spent. If your fleet runs at mean sharing factor N̄, your utilisation dashboards are inflated by N̄× on exactly the nodes management is watching for efficiency wins.

The one-line decision

Need isolation, an SLO, or a defensible per-tenant bill → MIG. Need throughput from jobs that under-fill the device, and the tenants trust each other → MPS. Need simple density on trusted, bursty, low-stakes work → time-slicing.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

You enable 4-way time-slicing to improve efficiency. What happens to your cost dashboard?

It over-reports by exactly 4× — +300% — if it uses a naive sum over DCGM series. The factor is N, independent of actual load, and it does not average out.

The perverse part: the harder you share to save money, the more your report overstates spend.

Which identity survives sharing, and which does not?

The scheduling identity survives: annotated GPU-<uuid>::N IDs come through the pod-resources API intact, so you always know which pod holds which replica.

The measurement identity does not: DCGM programs counters per physical device, so one value fans out across every holder.

A pod OOMs on a time-sliced GPU. Whose fault is it?

Probably not the pod that reported the error. There is no memory cap, so a neighbour can consume the whole framebuffer — and the OOM surfaces in the victim's logs.

This is why time-slicing is only appropriate for trusted, cooperative workloads.

Does time-slicing improve GPU throughput?

No. Turns are serialised, so there are no idle GPU-seconds between them beyond switch overhead — but each turn still wastes whatever fraction of the SM array the resident job leaves unused.

It packs the time axis. Only MPS packs occupancy.

Why is the fair-share estimator worse under time-slicing than under MPS?

Because time-slicing has no enforced ceilings. Actual shares are unbounded — one pod at 98% makes an even 4-way split wrong by 4×.

MPS's control daemon sets a default active-thread percentage and pinned-memory limit per client, both driver-enforced, so the same estimator becomes bounded and you can state the worst-case error.

What to keep

  • Time, not occupancySerialised turns. No concurrency gain; the SM array stays under-filled.
  • Three absencesNo memory cap, no fault isolation, no concurrency. All three are the mechanism, not a bug.
  • Scheduling survives, measurement does notAnnotated ::N IDs are intact; DCGM counters are per-device.
  • Naive sum over-reports ×NIndependent of load, does not average out, scales with the sharing factor.
  • Fair share is unbounded hereNo ceilings, so no error bound. This is what MPS fixes.
  • Trusted tenants onlyA device-wide fault domain and a free-for-all framebuffer rule out multi-tenant use.
