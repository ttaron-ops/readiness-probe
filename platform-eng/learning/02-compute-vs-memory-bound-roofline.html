Two independent physical facts bound how fast any kernel can finish. The roofline is what you get when you plot both on the same axes — and the single most useful number in GPU performance work is where they cross.

Part onethe model

Two ceilings, one graph

Arithmetic intensity — and the word that matters

AI = FLOPs performed ÷ bytes moved to and from DRAM, in FLOP/byte.

Williams, Waterman and Patterson define it as operations per byte of traffic between the processor and off-chip DRAM. Bytes served from L1 or L2 do not count — which means AI is a property of the algorithm and its implementation and the cache size, not of the mathematics alone. That is why "fusion raises arithmetic intensity" is a mechanism and not a slogan.

Plot AI on a log x-axis against attainable FLOP/s on a log y-axis. The memory bound AI × B becomes a slanted line of slope 1; the compute bound P is flat. Where they meet is the ridge point:

Log–log, both axes in decades. The slanted amber line is HBM bandwidth — its slope is fixed at 3.35 TB/s. Each compute roof has its own ridge point, because each divides a different P by the same B. Every point in the left-hand wedge has a ceiling of AI × 3.35 TB/s and nothing else can raise it.

Moving a point
  • → right = fuse kernels
  • → right = batch harder
  • → right = block/tile for reuse
  • all three shrink DRAM bytes per FLOP
Moving a roof
  • ↑ up = lower precision (BF16→FP8)
  • ↑ up = newer SKU
  • ↗ tilt = more bandwidth (H100→H200)
  • only helps if you can reach it
The lower roofs are a diagnostic ordering

A kernel that only uses FP32 CUDA cores is capped at 67 TFLOP/s no matter how high its AI — so its ridge sits at AI = 20. This is the original paper's ceilings idea: below the topmost roof are lower roofs for instruction mixes the kernel fails to exploit (2009: no SIMD, no FMA; 2026: no tensor cores, no FP8, no 2:4 sparsity).

Their value is ordering your work: if you are under the FP32 ceiling, switching to tensor cores is worth more than any bandwidth optimisation, and no amount of bandwidth will help you.

The caveat that is the entire point of cuBLAS

N/3 assumes each matrix is read from DRAM exactly once, which requires blocking so tiles stay resident in shared memory and registers. A naive triple loop re-reads a row of A and a column of B for every output element, giving AI ≈ 0.5 FLOP/byte.

Same mathematics, same FLOPs, 5,000× difference in arithmetic intensity at N=8192 — entirely from data reuse. This is why AI is a property of the implementation.

The formal version of two things you already say

"Prefill is compute-bound" = AI is S/2, which passes the ridge at S ≈ 590 and keeps climbing.

"Decode is memory-bound" = AI is ≈1 FLOP/byte at batch 1, ~295× to the left of the ridge. That is the same arithmetic that pins GPU_UTIL at 100% in lesson 05.1.

In twenty years the ridge point moved from 1 to ~300 FLOP/byte. That single trend is why "memory-bound" is the default condition of modern accelerated computing, and why so much of ML systems engineering is really data-movement engineering.

The H200 row is the interesting one: it is the only entry that moves the ridge point left relative to its predecessor, because NVIDIA raised bandwidth while holding compute fixed. A lower ridge point means more workloads land in the compute-bound region — i.e. more of them can actually use the FLOPs you are paying for.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

What is the H100's BF16 ridge point, and what does it physically mean?

989e12 ÷ 3.35e12 ≈ 295 FLOP/byte. It is the arithmetic intensity at which the chip's two capabilities are exactly matched.

Below it, the memory system is the binding constraint and no kernel — however well written — can reach the compute roof. Above it you may be compute-bound; a bad kernel still underperforms both roofs.

A SAXPY kernel gets 0.56 TFLOP/s on an H100. Is it badly written?

No — that is exactly its ceiling. AI = 0.167, so attainable = 0.167 × 3.35e12 = 5.6e11 FLOP/s. It is achieving 100% of what the roofline permits, which happens to be 0.057% of peak compute.

Buying a faster-compute GPU raises a roof this kernel never touches. Only more bandwidth, or a higher-AI formulation, helps.

Why does FlashAttention help so much, in roofline terms?

It moves the point right, not up. Fused: AI = S/2 = 2,048 at S=4096. Unfused: AI = d/4 = 32, because the S×S score matrix round-trips to HBM and 16·S² (268 MB) dominates the 8·S·d (4.2 MB) of real data.

Identical FLOPs, 64× the arithmetic intensity — crossing from memory-bound to compute-bound.

Naive GEMM gets AI ≈ 0.5 instead of N/3. What changed?

Only the denominator. A naive triple loop re-reads a row of A and a column of B from DRAM for every output element, so bytes scale as N³ rather than N². The numerator — 2N³ FLOPs — is unchanged.

At N=8192 that is a 5,000× difference in AI from data reuse alone. This is why arithmetic intensity is a property of the implementation, and why you should never quote an AI without saying whether it assumes blocking.

Your kernel sits under the FP32 ceiling. What do you fix first?

Use the tensor cores. Under the FP32 roof you are capped at 67 TFLOP/s regardless of arithmetic intensity, so no amount of bandwidth or fusion work will raise you above it.

That is what the lower roofs are for: they order your optimisation work rather than merely describing it.

Why is the H200 an unusual row in the SKU table?

It is the only one that moves the ridge point left — from 295 to 206 — because bandwidth rose (3.35 → 4.8 TB/s) while dense BF16 compute stayed at 989 TFLOP/s.

Practically: a lower ridge means more of your workloads land compute-bound, so more of the FLOPs you are renting are actually reachable.
