The only sharing mode that reclaims wasted SM capacity, and the only one whose attribution estimate comes with a worst case you can state.

Axis occupancy, not time Ceilings 100/replicas, driver-enforced Maturity a three-clause answer

MPS is the sharing mode most engineers misplace — either unaware it exists, or reaching for the wrong mechanism when asked how it differs from time-slicing. It is the only mode that reclaims wasted SM capacity, and the only one that turns fair-share attribution from a guess into a bounded estimate.

Part onethe axis

Packing occupancy, not time

Time-slicing packs

The time axis. No idle GPU-seconds between turns — but each turn still wastes whatever fraction of the SM array the resident job does not need.

MPS packs

The occupancy axis. Kernels from different processes execute concurrently on different SMs. On workloads that under-fill the device, that is a direct throughput multiplier.

“One shared CUDA context” is the pre-Volta model

And it is the single most common inaccuracy about MPS. Volta changed both the work-submission path and the address-space model: clients submit work to the hardware themselves, and each has its own GPU address space.

MPS is three distinct processes — a control daemon, a per-user server, and the clients — not one context everyone shares.

The attribution upgrade almost nobody mentions

Because those ceilings are driver-enforced, fair share stops being a shrug. Under time-slicing a pod can take 98% of the device and your even split is wrong by 4×, with no bound. Under MPS the per-client error is bounded by u/N and is signed — you can state a worst case.

That is the difference between a number a FinOps lead will accept and one they will not.

Part threerisk

The fault domain, more precisely than the folklore

“MPS has the worst fault domain” is received wisdom and it is too crude. Volta and later contained a fatal fault to the set of clients sharing the offending GPU, rather than every client of the server across all GPUs. That is narrower than pre-Volta.

It is still an all-clients-on-that-GPU kill, plus a failure mode time-slicing does not have at all: the control daemon and per-user server are processes that can die, hang, or be left in a fault state. The manual warns that a runaway kernel may require a forced server shutdown, and that terminating a client without synchronising its GPU work can leave the server and other clients undefined.

Narrower than the folklore, still disqualifying for untrusted tenants. Getting that nuance right is what a design review is testing.

The maturity answer has three clauses

MPS sharing is marked experimental in NVIDIA's device plugin since v0.15.0 and remains so through the current v0.19.x line; it is not supported on MIG-enabled devices by that plugin; and in the DRA driver it sits behind an alpha, default-off feature gate.

Note the precision on the middle clause: the MIG incompatibility is a device-plugin limitation, not a hardware one — the DRA driver models MPS on MIG devices explicitly via MigDeviceSharing with an MpsConfig. “MPS and MIG are mutually exclusive, full stop” is the kind of absolute that gets falsified in the follow-up question.

The one-line decision

Need isolation, an SLO, or a defensible per-tenant bill → MIG. Need throughput from jobs that under-fill the device, and the tenants trust each other → MPS. Need simple density on trusted, bursty, low-stakes work → time-slicing.

The published envelope is narrow, and that is useful

Databricks measured two identical inference engines on one H100 across the Qwen2.5 family and found the win confined to very small models (≈≤3B) at short-to-medium context. A negative result outside a narrow band is far more useful for policy than a success story.

Pebble reported Qwen3-4B-FP8 on an A100-80GB in GKE at 60% SM / 50% memory per process, two processes per GPU: ~50% cost reduction for ~7.5% throughput loss.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

What does MPS share that time-slicing does not?

The occupancy axis. Kernels from different processes run concurrently on different SMs, so a job that lights only a fraction of the SM array no longer wastes the rest during its turn.

Time-slicing packs time; there are no idle GPU-seconds between turns, but each turn still under-fills the device.

“MPS uses one shared CUDA context.” Correct?

That is the pre-Volta model, and it is the most common inaccuracy about MPS. From Volta, clients submit work to the hardware themselves and each has its own GPU address space.

MPS is three processes: control daemon, per-user server, clients.

Why is fair-share attribution better under MPS than under time-slicing?

Because the control daemon sets a default active-thread percentage (100/replicas) and a pinned-memory limit (totalMemory/replicas), both driver-enforced. They are ceilings, so a client can under-use but not exceed its share.

That makes the per-client error bounded by u/N and signed, instead of unbounded. You can state a worst case rather than shrug.

Is MPS's fault domain the worst of the three?

More precisely than the folklore: from Volta a fatal fault is contained to the clients sharing that GPU, which is narrower than pre-Volta. But it is still an all-clients-on-that-GPU kill, plus the daemon and per-user server are extra processes that can die or hang.

Narrower than the received wisdom, still disqualifying for untrusted tenants.

“MPS and MIG are mutually exclusive.” True?

Only of the device plugin, which does not support MPS on MIG-enabled devices. It is not a hardware limitation — the DRA driver models MPS on MIG explicitly through MigDeviceSharing with an MpsConfig.

Though note the DRA path itself is behind an alpha, default-off gate.

Is MPS production-ready?

Three clauses: experimental in NVIDIA's device plugin since v0.15.0 and still so at v0.19.x; unsupported on MIG-enabled devices by that plugin; alpha and default-off in the DRA driver.

Delivering all three cleanly is the answer — "no" and "yes" are both wrong.
