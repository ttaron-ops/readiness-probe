A job is a block, not a quantity — so free GPUs scattered across nodes place nothing. The gap has a closed form, and it collapses faster than anyone expects.

Closed form (1−u)^(G−1) At 60% utilisation ~0.0017 of the naive estimate Always report the table, never one number

An allocated fleet is not a usable fleet. The gap is not waste in the ordinary sense — the GPUs are free, powered, and idle — they simply cannot form a whole job. This page is about computing that gap instead of assuming it away.

The whole lesson in one sentence

The first two are scalars over a fungible pool. The third is not, because the thing you are placing is not a scalar.

A distributed job needs k GPUs that are simultaneously present (the collective barrier means N−1 ranks make zero progress), atomically admitted, and co-located in a topology domain. So the request is not "k GPUs from the fleet" — it is a contiguous block of k GPUs from one node or domain, all free at the same instant.

You cannot run six of it here and two there. That is bin-packing: an item that fits in no single bin does not run, however much total free space exists.

Part twothe closed form

Why spread placement collapses

Model it. Nodes have G GPUs; each GPU is independently occupied with probability u; jobs need k co-located GPUs. Independence is the "no packing logic at all" case — what you get from placement that ignores node fullness — so it is a lower bound on what a real scheduler achieves.

G = 8 GPUs per node. Solid lines are the honest expectation for each job size; the dashed line is the naive fungible estimate for a whole-node job. Small jobs track the naive line closely — fragmentation is a large-job problem, which is exactly why it shows up when you start training rather than when you start serving.

At a utilisation most platform teams would call comfortable, a fleet has 3.2 free GPUs per node on average — and can place 0.0007 whole-node jobs per node against a naive estimate of 0.40. That is a ratio of about 0.0017.

Your dashboard says 40% free. Your 8-GPU job says Pending. Both are correct.

Always report the table, never a single number

Fragmentation is job-size dependent. "The fleet is 12% fragmented" is meaningless without saying which job shape you measured. The same inventory is nearly perfect for k=1 and nearly useless for k=8.

Part threethe lever

Spread versus bin-pack

Spread placement
  • Default scoring leans this way — balance load across nodes
  • Every node ends up partly full
  • Free GPUs scatter into unusable singletons
  • The model above is this case
Bin-pack placement
  • Fill nodes before starting new ones
  • Keeps whole nodes empty and placeable
  • Costs you blast-radius concentration
  • Recovers most of the gap
Which is a Score, not a Filter

Straight back to lesson 02.9: packing preference belongs in the Score stage. Written as a Filter, a fleet with no perfectly-packed node available would leave jobs Pending rather than placing them imperfectly.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

Your dashboard says 40% of GPUs are free. How many 8-GPU jobs can you place?

Unanswerable from that number alone — and if you compute it naively you get 0.40 per node, which is wrong by roughly 500×.

With 8-GPU nodes at 60% utilisation and spread placement, the expectation is (1−u)8 ≈ 0.00066 whole-node jobs per node. You need Σ floor(free_i / k) over actual nodes, not a fleet total.

Give the closed form for whole-node jobs and explain the exponent.

E[placeable(G)] = N(1−u)G, versus a naive N(1−u), so the surviving fraction is (1−u)G−1.

The exponent is G because floor(f/G) is 1 only when all G GPUs on the node are free — every one of them must independently be free, and those probabilities multiply.

Someone reports "the fleet is 12% fragmented." What do you ask?

"For which job size?" Fragmentation is job-size dependent — the same inventory is nearly perfect for k=1 and nearly useless for k=8.

Always report the table across job shapes, never a single scalar.

Why is the independence assumption a lower bound rather than an estimate?

Because independent occupancy is the "no packing logic at all" case. Any scheduler that biases toward filling partly-used nodes does better than random, so the model floors your expectation rather than predicting it.

That makes it useful for arguing a change is needed, and useless for promising a specific post-change number.

Why does fragmentation appear when you add training, not when you add inference?

Because it is a large-k phenomenon. Single-GPU inference pods track the naive estimate closely; an 8-GPU gang needs every GPU on a node free simultaneously.

The fleet did not change — the job shape did.

Bin-packing recovers the capacity. Why not just always bin-pack?

It concentrates blast radius: a node failure takes down more co-located work, and a noisy neighbour has more neighbours. It also fights spread-based availability goals.

And per lesson 02.9 it belongs in Score, not Filter — as a preference, so a fleet with no well-packed node still places the job.
