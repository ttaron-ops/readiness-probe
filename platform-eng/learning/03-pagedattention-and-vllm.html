Module 07 · 07.3 · Inference serving

The block table
is a page table

Not an analogy. Refcounts, copy-on-write, LRU eviction and page faults all appear, doing the same jobs — and the waste stops scaling with context length.

Fixes 80% fragmentation → under 4% Cost indirection on every attention step Follows lesson 07.2 — the KV budget

Lesson 07.2 ended with a contiguous allocator wasting 80% of the pool. The fix is not a better allocator — it is deleting the requirement that made every allocator bad. The block table is a page table, and the correspondence is exact rather than metaphorical.

Part oneelimination

Why every obvious fix fails

Knowing why the easy answers fail is what makes paging feel inevitable rather than clever.

“Just…”Why it does not work
…allocate the actual lengthYou do not know it. The model emits EOS when it decides to. You can cap with max_tokens, but callers set that generously for exactly the same reason allocators over-reserve.
…grow the buffer with reallocGrowing means copying, and you are copying hundreds of MB of KV on the GPU inside a decode step with a 5 ms budget. One 8k-context 70B sequence is 2.5 GiB — 1.5 ms round-trip at 3.35 TB/s, per growth, per sequence.
…compact the pool like a moving GCSame objection at larger scale, plus you must stop the world across every in-flight sequence to update every kernel's base pointers.
…set max_model_len lowThis works and you should right-size it — but it caps the product, not the waste. At 2048 you still hold ~80% dead reservation, and you have also started refusing long requests.
Every failure points the same direction

The problem is the requirement of contiguity, not the size of the allocation. Break contiguity and all four objections dissolve at once. That is the entire idea.

  1. Near-zero fragmentation

    The only waste left is the partially filled last block of each sequence — bounded by block_size − 1 tokens per sequence forever, however long it grows. The paper reports under 4% waste against 60–80%. External fragmentation is not reduced, it is eliminated: uniform blocks mean there is no such thing as a hole of the wrong shape.

  2. On-demand growth

    A sequence takes what its prompt needs, then one more block every 16 generated tokens. This is what turns 07.2's worst-case 48 concurrent into a measured 97 on the same pool.

  3. Admission without a search

    The scheduler admits when enough free blocks exist anywhere — a counter comparison, not a search for a contiguous run. That is what physically permits a high max-num-seqs.

Part threethe kernel

Why this is a paper, not a config flag

This is the part most explanations skip. Standard attention for a query at position t walks K[0], K[1], … K[t-1] at regular address increments — wide, coalesced, prefetchable reads, exactly what a GPU's memory subsystem is built for.

Under paging, positions 0–15 live in one physical block, 16–31 in another, and those blocks are anywhere. So for each logical block i the kernel must look up block_table[i], compute a physical base address, and read 16 positions from there.

Breaking contiguity is free in the allocator and expensive in the kernel

You are trading a memory-layout problem for an indirection problem, and the indirection has to be paid on every attention step of every sequence. That is why PagedAttention arrived as a paper with a custom kernel rather than a patch to an allocator — and why "just make the cache non-contiguous" was not a thing anyone could do before.

Part foursharing

Content-addressed blocks

Two sequences can share a block only if they agree its contents are identical — and identical tokens do not imply identical KV, because K and V depend on everything before them through attention. The fix is hash chaining.

RuleConsequence in practice
Only full blocks are cached A partial tail block has no stable identity — its remaining slots will be written by tokens not yet decided. So prefix hits land on block_size boundaries: a 2,000-token shared system prompt hits on 1,984 tokens and re-prefills the last 16.
The hash default changed to SHA-256 A deliberate collision-safety decision. A collision in a multi-tenant deployment means one tenant's request reads another tenant's KV — a data-leak class of bug, not a performance bug. xxhash is faster; sha256_cbor is reproducible across languages.
cache_salt isolates tenants Closes a real timing side channel: without it an adversary can infer whether a prefix was seen recently by measuring TTFT. The cost is fragmenting the cache along salt boundaries — which is exactly the trade you want.

The multiplier depends entirely on your traffic

Anyscale's naive static baseline collapsed to 81 tokens/second on OPT-13B once realistic output-length variance was introduced, and continuous batching reached up to 23× that on the same 40 GB A100. Orca (OSDI '22) reported 36.9× at matched latency versus FasterTransformer on GPT-3 175B.

Note the framing: with fixed-length outputs static batching is fine. It is the variance that destroys it — so your multiplier is a property of your length distribution, not of the software.

Part sixrunning dry

Preemption is recomputation, not suspension

A preempted 8k-context request pays a second full 8k prefill

num_computed_tokens = 0 means resumption re-runs the entire prefill. That is why preemptions hit TTFT so hard, and why vllm:num_preemptions_total climbing alongside a latency spike is one problem, not two.

Prefix caching softens this materially: the recompute is a fresh prefill that goes through get_computed_blocks like any other, and the request's own blocks were only just freed — so unless they have been evicted they are still hashed and hittable. An under-appreciated second-order benefit of default-on APC.

Using itdrills

Drill yourself

Why can't you just realloc the KV buffer as the sequence grows?

Growing means copying, and the copy is hundreds of MB to gigabytes of GPU memory inside a decode step with roughly a 5 ms budget. One 8k-context 70B sequence is 2.5 GiB — about 1.5 ms round-trip at 3.35 TB/s, per growth, per sequence.

How much memory does paging waste per sequence, and how does it scale with context length?

At most block_size − 1 = 15 tokens, in the partially filled last block. It does not scale at all — it is a constant per sequence regardless of how long the sequence grows, which is why it is ~1% at realistic lengths against 80% for contiguous.

External fragmentation is not reduced but structurally eliminated: uniform blocks mean every free block is interchangeable.

Two requests share a 2,000-token system prompt. How many tokens hit the prefix cache?

1,984 — floor(2000/16) × 16. Only full blocks are cached, because a partial tail block has no stable identity: its remaining slots will be written by tokens not yet decided.

The last 16 tokens are re-prefilled.

Why does a block's hash include its parent's hash?

Because identical tokens do not imply identical KV — K and V depend on the entire preceding context through attention. Chaining makes a block's identity depend on its whole prefix, so block 3 of one document cannot collide with block 3 of a different document that happens to end with the same words.

vllm:num_preemptions_total is climbing and p99 TTFT spiked. One problem or two?

One. Preemption sets num_computed_tokens = 0, so a preempted request re-runs its entire prefill on resumption. It is recomputation, not suspension.

With prefix caching on, much of that reprefill may hit cache — the victim's blocks were only just freed and are still hashed unless evicted.

When is static batching actually fine?

When output lengths are uniform. The collapse is caused by variance: a 20-token reply batched with a 2,000-token reply leaves its slot idle for 1,980 steps.

So the throughput multiplier you get from continuous batching is a property of your traffic's length distribution, not of the serving engine.

  • Contiguity was the bugNot allocation size. All four obvious fixes fail for the same underlying reason.
  • Block table = page tableThe correspondence is exact, down to refcounts, copy-on-write, and LRU eviction.
